Markets crash when many investors try to sell at once, but there aren’t enough willing buyers at recent prices. That imbalance can cause prices to fall rapidly.

Common causes include:

- **Overvaluation:** Prices become detached from earnings, cash flows, or economic fundamentals.
- **A sudden shock:** Examples include a financial failure, war, pandemic, policy change, or disappointing economic data.
- **Rising interest rates:** Higher rates make borrowing more expensive and reduce the present value of future profits, especially for growth stocks.
- **Leverage and forced selling:** Investors who borrowed money may be forced to sell when prices fall, accelerating the decline.
- **Loss of confidence:** Fear can spread quickly, causing investors to sell simply because others are selling.
- **Liquidity problems:** In stressed markets, buyers may withdraw, making even modest selling produce large price drops.
- **Contagion:** Problems in one company, sector, or country can spread through banks, funds, supply chains, and investor sentiment.
- **Programmed trading and derivatives:** Stop-loss orders, margin calls, and automated strategies can amplify rapid moves.

A typical crash involves a feedback loop:

**Bad news → selling → falling prices → margin calls or fear → more selling.**

Crashes are usually not caused by one factor alone. They often occur after a period of excessive optimism, when markets are highly valued, heavily leveraged, or unusually dependent on a particular assumption. They are difficult to predict precisely, although underlying vulnerabilities can sometimes be identified.
