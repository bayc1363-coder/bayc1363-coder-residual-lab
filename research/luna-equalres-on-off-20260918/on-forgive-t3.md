## Frame A — Recovery and agency

Frame B is weakened if its language of mercy increases the injured person’s burden rather than their freedom: harm → pressure to preserve the offender’s worth → pressure to relinquish anger → reduced willingness to protect oneself or demand consequences. The decisive test is not whether mercy feels psychologically helpful, but whether the practice actually returns agency to the injured person. If “forgive” functions as social pressure to resume contact, soften testimony, or stop naming the wrong, Frame B has become a tool of control.

## Frame B — Mercy and moral repair

Frame B would count against itself if its distinction between person and offense cannot sustain accountability in practice: wrongdoing → refusal of revenge → demand for truth and repair → consequences when repair is refused. It fails normatively when mercy excuses rather than interrupts retaliation, treats reconciliation as owed without remorse, or makes the victim carry the moral work while the offender keeps the benefits of impunity. It also faces a coherence problem if it claims to preserve the wrongdoer’s dignity while disregarding the victim’s equal dignity. Those are objections to the frame’s moral structure and social consequences, not diagnoses that the victim has failed to heal.

## Residual

No frame alone determines the right penalty, the credibility of claimed remorse, or the institutional safeguards needed when private forgiveness could weaken public accountability.
