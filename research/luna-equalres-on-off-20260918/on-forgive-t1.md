## Frame A — Recovery and agency

Forgiveness can help because an injury often keeps reproducing itself through rumination, anger, and fantasies of retaliation. By deliberately releasing the demand to keep punishing the offender, a person can recover attention, emotional freedom, and control over their next actions. This does not require denying the harm, restoring trust, or abandoning boundaries; it means refusing to let the offense remain the organizing force of one’s life.

## Frame B — Mercy and moral repair

Forgiveness can matter because wrongdoing damages a relationship without making the wrongdoer identical to the wrongdoing. By distinguishing accountability from revenge, the injured person can condemn the act, require restitution or change, and still leave open the possibility of repentance and restored human regard. In this frame, forgiveness interrupts the chain of injury and retaliation, while justice and safety remain necessary conditions for reconciliation.

## Residual

Neither frame determines whether forgiveness is timely, safe, or owed; severe abuse may require distance, protection, or consequences before any release is possible.

## Working answer

Someone should forgive when doing so can both loosen the injury’s control over their life and prevent justice from hardening into revenge—but forgiveness should be chosen at a safe pace, without erasing the harm, surrendering boundaries, or pretending reconciliation has already been earned.
