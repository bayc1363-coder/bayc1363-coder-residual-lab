## Frame A — Recovery without permission

If the other person is not sorry, forgiveness can still proceed as an act of self-protection: name the harm accurately → stop waiting for acknowledgment → release the demand that the offender repair your inner life → maintain whatever distance and boundaries safety requires. Their lack of remorse may rule out trust or reconciliation, but it need not give them permanent control over your attention.

## Frame B — Mercy without absolution

If the other person is not sorry, forgiveness can still proceed as a refusal to make repentance the condition of their basic human worth: distinguish the person from the offense → relinquish personal vengeance → leave justice, consequences, and any possibility of repentance in place → withhold reconciliation until truthful remorse and changed conduct appear. Forgiveness here does not declare the act acceptable or the offender trustworthy.

## Residual

Neither frame settles whether contact is safe, whether consequences should be pursued, or whether “forgiveness” is the right word; in some cases, protection and firm non-reconciliation are the necessary next steps.

## Working answer

When the other person is not sorry, forgiveness can still be worthwhile as both release from dependence on their remorse and a refusal to let vengeance define the response—but it does not require trust, contact, absolution, or reconciliation.
