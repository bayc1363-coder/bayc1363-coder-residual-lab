If by **Frame B** you mean the view that forgiveness is primarily something the injured person can do for their own freedom, even without the offender’s remorse, then what counts against it would be more than “forgiveness feels difficult.”

Good objections would include:

1. **Conceptual ambiguity:** If “forgiveness” is reduced to letting go, acceptance, or moving on, the frame may be changing the meaning of forgiveness rather than explaining it. Forgiveness may plausibly require some attitude toward the wrongdoer, not merely emotional self-regulation.

2. **Moral objection:** The frame could understate the importance of accountability. In some cases, emphasizing the victim’s release may make it easier to overlook ongoing danger, injustice, or the offender’s responsibility.

3. **Agency objection:** It may imply that people can simply choose to stop being affected. If forgiveness is not fully voluntary or available on demand, presenting it as a personal decision can unfairly burden the injured person.

4. **Counterexamples:** There are cases where refusing forgiveness is not vindictive but morally appropriate—for example, where forgiveness would weaken necessary boundaries, compromise justice, or communicate undeserved trust.

5. **Practical evidence:** If, in a given context, attempts to forgive reliably lead people to suppress anger, return to unsafe relationships, or abandon legitimate claims, that would count against the frame’s practical usefulness—not necessarily against forgiveness in every form.

To avoid turning this into **failed psychology**, don’t claim that forgiveness universally heals, liberates, or improves well-being. Treat those as contingent possibilities, and separate three questions:

- Is forgiveness psychologically helpful?
- Is it morally appropriate?
- Is it required or owed?

Evidence against one claim need not refute the others.
