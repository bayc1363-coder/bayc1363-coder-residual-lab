## Frame A — Fragility and forced selling

Markets crash when a fragile financial structure turns a manageable loss into a liquidity spiral:

1. Investors accumulate leverage, concentrated positions, or assets funded with short-term borrowing.  
2. A shock—bad earnings, a default, rate increase, geopolitical event, or policy surprise—reduces asset prices.  
3. Losses trigger margin calls, redemptions, or internal risk limits.  
4. Investors sell to raise cash, pushing prices below fundamental estimates.  
5. Falling prices create further losses and collateral shortages, producing more forced selling and temporarily impaired market liquidity.

On this frame, crashes are produced less by the initial news than by the balance-sheet feedback that follows it.

## Frame B — Repricing of a shared economic story

Markets crash when a widely held valuation regime loses credibility and investors revise their collective view of future returns:

1. Prices embody shared assumptions about growth, inflation, interest rates, profits, policy, or risk.  
2. New information or a policy change conflicts with those assumptions.  
3. Investors revise expected cash flows, discount rates, or acceptable risk premia.  
4. Because many portfolios are positioned around the same story, repositioning becomes synchronized.  
5. Prices gap downward, weakening confidence and investment expectations, which can then affect the economy that markets are trying to forecast.

On this frame, leverage and forced sales are important means, but the deeper cause is a coordinated shift in what assets are expected to be worth.

## Residual

Neither frame alone determines the exact trigger, timing, scale, or whether the crash reflects justified information, temporary panic, institutional design, or some combination; evidence may also be incomplete.

## Working answer

Markets crash when a change in expectations meets a financial structure that cannot absorb rapid repositioning: investors revise the value of assets, and leverage, correlated positions, margin calls, and forced selling turn that revision into a self-reinforcing fall.
