If a regulator is captured by the industry it oversees, regulation can become ineffective—or even serve the industry’s interests while appearing legitimate. That is a serious risk, but it generally argues for improving institutional design rather than abandoning regulation.

Safeguards include:

- **Transparency:** Publish meetings, lobbying contacts, regulatory data, impact assessments, and enforcement records.
- **Conflict-of-interest rules:** Restrict officials from owning relevant financial interests or immediately joining companies they regulated.
- **Cooling-off periods:** Prevent regulators and industry executives from moving directly between government and the firms they oversee.
- **Independent funding and leadership:** Reduce dependence on industry fees or political pressure, and use fixed terms for agency heads where appropriate.
- **Public and scientific participation:** Include environmental groups, affected communities, independent researchers, and local governments—not only industry representatives.
- **Strong enforcement and judicial review:** Allow courts, legislatures, inspectors general, and citizen groups to challenge weak enforcement.
- **Whistleblower protections:** Protect employees who report concealed pollution or regulatory misconduct.
- **Multiple checks:** Divide authority among agencies or levels of government so one captured institution cannot control the entire process.
- **Automatic reviews:** Require regulations and permits to be periodically reassessed using publicly available evidence.

Other tools—such as pollution disclosure, civil liability, citizen lawsuits, and market-based incentives—can supplement direct regulation, though they too require oversight.

Industry expertise is often necessary for competent regulation, so the goal is not to exclude industry entirely. It is to ensure that industry advice is balanced by independent evidence and public accountability. If capture remains pervasive, stronger democratic oversight and institutional reform are essential.
