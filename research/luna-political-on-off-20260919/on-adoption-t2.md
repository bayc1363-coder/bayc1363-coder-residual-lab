## Frame A — Preference within equal eligibility

The child’s wish for a mother and a father should matter, but it should not automatically disqualify a same-sex couple.

1. Children’s preferences can reflect genuine needs, prior experiences, cultural expectations, or ideas absorbed from adults.  
2. Adoption professionals should therefore ask what the child means by “a mother and a father”: a desire for particular caregiving qualities, reassurance about identity, continuity with siblings, or simply a familiar family image.  
3. Those needs should be addressed directly through preparation, counseling, contact with trusted adults, and careful matching where feasible.  
4. The applicants should still be assessed on safety, attachment, permanence, and ability to meet this child’s needs—not on the assumption that one family structure automatically supplies them.

## Frame B — Child-specific fit and agency

The child’s expressed preference should carry greater weight when the child is older, understands the alternatives, and has a stable reason for it.

1. Adoption decisions should begin with the child’s welfare and developing agency, rather than with an adult claim to equal access.  
2. A stated wish for a mother and a father should be explored for its source, persistence, and connection to the child’s history and emotional security.  
3. If the preference identifies a serious, child-specific need that a proposed placement cannot meet, the placement should be rejected or reconsidered.  
4. If it mainly reflects a general expectation about families, it should inform preparation and matching but should not function as an automatic veto, especially where delay or institutional care would itself harm the child.

## Residual

The difficult judgment concerns the child’s age, maturity, reasons, available placements, and whether the preference is stable and welfare-related rather than a generalized expectation.

## Working answer

A child’s wish for a mother and a father should be taken seriously and investigated, but it should not by itself bar a same-sex couple; the decisive question is whether the specific placement best meets that child’s enduring needs.
