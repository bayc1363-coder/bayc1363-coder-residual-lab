## Frame A — Retributive justice and public protection

If DNA evidence makes guilt effectively certain, the strongest objection to execution—wrongful identification—largely disappears. Under this frame:

- The evidence establishes that the defendant committed the exceptionally serious act, rather than merely creating a strong inference.
- The court can then focus on aggravating and mitigating circumstances, including intent, scale of harm, vulnerability of victims, and the possibility of future danger.
- For a crime such as deliberate mass murder, execution may be viewed as proportionate condemnation, not merely as a method of incapacitation.
- DNA certainty does not by itself prove that death is deserved: the evidence must be authentic, properly handled, and sufficient to establish the required mental state.
- The punishment should therefore remain available only after full procedural review, especially because a certain perpetrator can still receive an excessive or discriminatory sentence.

## Frame B — Human rights and limited necessity

If DNA makes guilt certain, it answers the factual question of who committed the crime, but not whether the state should execute that person.

- DNA can establish identity or physical involvement, while separate evidence may still be needed to prove intent, coercion, degree of participation, or the appropriate punishment.
- Even with guilt fixed, prosecutors and judges may apply aggravating factors inconsistently, and unequal access to counsel can affect sentencing.
- Life imprisonment can permanently protect the public without making an irreversible killing part of the legal system.
- The possibility of correction becomes less important when identity is certain, but the moral objection remains: punishment may condemn the act without reproducing it.
- This frame therefore treats DNA certainty as a reason to narrow the dispute, not as a sufficient reason to authorize execution.

## Residual

The remaining question is whether DNA certainty extends beyond identity to every legally relevant fact, and whether execution is justified as retribution when life imprisonment provides comparable public protection.

## Working answer

Even when DNA evidence makes guilt effectively certain, the death penalty should remain unavailable as a general legal option: certainty strengthens the retributive case, but it does not resolve proportionality, unequal sentencing, or whether deliberate execution is necessary.
