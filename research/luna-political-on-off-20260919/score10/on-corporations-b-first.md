## Frame B — Liability and market discipline

Corporations should not generally be subject to expanding, sector-specific environmental commands when ordinary legal and market institutions can protect the environment. Environmental harm begins when a company’s activities impose costs on others; property rules, tort liability, disclosure duties, insurance, investor pressure, and customer choice can identify those harms; courts and insurers can then impose costs on polluters; those costs encourage cleaner production, technological innovation, and prevention. This approach limits bureaucratic discretion and lets firms discover different ways to reduce harm while preserving accountability for demonstrable damage.

Its pressure point is that environmental harms often cross borders, accumulate slowly, involve many victims, and are difficult for private plaintiffs to prove. Where ownership is unclear or damages are diffuse, liability and market signals can arrive too late. Even this frame therefore supports strong general rules against fraud, concealment, and clearly attributable pollution.

## Frame A — Public environmental regulation

Corporations should be regulated to protect the environment because profit-seeking firms can shift pollution, resource depletion, and climate risks onto the public. Environmental damage begins when production creates costs that firms do not bear; governments establish standards, monitoring systems, permits, taxes, or emissions limits; enforcement makes compliance more predictable and costly violations less attractive; firms then redesign processes, adopt cleaner technologies, and reduce harm before it becomes irreversible. Regulation can also coordinate action across industries and prevent responsible firms from being undercut by less responsible competitors.

Its pressure point is poor design: rigid rules can suppress innovation, favor large incumbents, or impose costs without addressing the most serious risks. Effective regulation therefore needs measurable goals, transparent enforcement, periodic revision, and room for firms to choose how to meet environmental targets.

## Working answer

Corporations should generally be regulated, while liability and market discipline should supplement—not replace—public rules where environmental harms are diffuse, cumulative, or difficult to litigate. Regulation is strongest for setting shared environmental floors and preventing irreversible damage; the rival frame is strongest for preserving flexibility, assigning responsibility for proven harms, and encouraging innovation beyond minimum standards. The best policy therefore combines enforceable limits, transparent monitoring, corporate disclosure, meaningful liability, and market incentives rather than relying exclusively on either command-and-control regulation or private discipline.
