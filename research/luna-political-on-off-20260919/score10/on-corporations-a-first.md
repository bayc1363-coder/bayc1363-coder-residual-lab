## Frame A — Public environmental protection

Corporations should be regulated because they can impose pollution, resource depletion, and climate costs on people who did not consent to bear them. The chain is:

1. Corporate production creates environmental harms.
2. Those harms become external costs when firms do not pay for them.
3. Voluntary restraint often loses against competitors that cut costs through pollution.
4. Enforceable standards, taxes, disclosure rules, and liability obligations make firms internalize environmental damage.
5. Internalized costs redirect investment toward cleaner production while preventing irreversible harm.

On this frame, regulation is not merely an administrative burden; it is the mechanism that aligns private incentives with public health and ecological limits.

## Frame B — Market discipline and bounded government

Corporations should not automatically face extensive environmental regulation because governments can misjudge risks, protect incumbent firms, and impose rigid rules that block cheaper innovations. The chain is:

1. Firms respond to prices, liability, consumer pressure, investor scrutiny, and reputational losses.
2. Clear property rights, pollution liability, environmental disclosure, and competition expose harmful practices to market and legal penalties.
3. Firms then develop cleaner processes because reducing environmental risk protects profits and market access.
4. Government action should supply information, enforce liability, and correct demonstrable market failures rather than prescribe every production method.
5. Targeted regulation remains necessary where harms are diffuse, long-term, catastrophic, or impossible for victims to litigate, but broad command-and-control regulation should not be the default.

On this frame, the central question is not whether corporations face constraints, but whether each constraint produces more environmental protection than its compliance costs and unintended effects.

## Residual

The frames do not settle which instruments work best in a particular sector, how strict standards should be, how reliably governments will enforce them, or whether regulation shifts pollution across borders.

## Working answer

Corporations should face enforceable environmental rules, but those rules should be targeted toward measurable harms and designed to preserve market incentives for innovation rather than dictate every production decision. Regulation is strongest where pollution creates costs that markets cannot reliably price, while liability, disclosure, competition, and consumer pressure can handle less severe or more readily observable harms. Overall, the case for regulation is strong, but its legitimacy depends on evidence that the chosen rule prevents more damage than it creates.
