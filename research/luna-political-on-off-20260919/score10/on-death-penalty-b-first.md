## Frame B — Irreversible-state power
The state should reject the death penalty even for the gravest crimes. First, courts can convict the wrong person through faulty evidence, misconduct, or inadequate representation. Second, execution makes correction impossible after an error. Third, death sentences are applied unevenly across jurisdictions and defendants, weakening equal justice. Fourth, life imprisonment without release can permanently protect the public and express condemnation while preserving the possibility of correcting a wrongful conviction. Therefore, the death penalty should not remain an option.

## Frame A — Proportionate desert and protection
The state should retain the death penalty as an option for exceptionally grave crimes such as intentional mass killing. First, deliberate killing on that scale can warrant the strongest proportionate condemnation. Second, a lawful execution can permanently incapacitate an offender and affirm the equal worth of victims. Third, retaining the option may serve retributive justice where imprisonment is judged insufficient. Fourth, the power should be limited to narrow offenses, exceptionally strong evidence, competent counsel, automatic appeals, and heightened review. Therefore, the death penalty should remain available in the rarest cases.

## Residual
The decisive unresolved issues are whether safeguards can reliably prevent wrongful execution and whether execution adds public-safety or deterrence benefits beyond permanent imprisonment.

## Working answer
The answer turns on whether proportionate condemnation and permanent incapacitation justify giving the state an irreversible power despite fallible courts and unequal application. The death penalty may therefore remain a narrowly constrained option only if evidence, representation, review, and administration can meet an exceptionally demanding standard; where that confidence is absent, life imprisonment is the stronger rule. On balance, it should not be a normal sentencing option, but a society that accepts the safeguards and moral premises in Frame A may reserve it for the rarest crimes.
