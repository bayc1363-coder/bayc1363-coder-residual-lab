## Frame B — Irreversible-state power
Even if DNA evidence makes guilt certain in a particular case, the death penalty should remain unavailable. First, certain identity does not establish that execution is necessary, proportionate, or morally permissible. Second, DNA may settle who committed the act without settling intent, coercion, mental capacity, or the reliability of the broader trial process. Third, execution preserves the state’s unequal power to impose an irreversible punishment across different defendants and jurisdictions. Fourth, permanent imprisonment can protect the public and express severe condemnation without closing every possibility of later legal or moral reconsideration. Therefore, DNA certainty removes one major objection but does not justify retaining execution.

## Frame A — Proportionate desert and protection
If DNA evidence makes guilt certain and the offense is among the most serious crimes, the state should retain the death penalty as an option. First, near-certain proof removes the strongest concern that an innocent person will be executed. Second, deliberate mass murder or comparable crimes can warrant the strongest proportionate condemnation. Third, execution permanently incapacitates the offender and can affirm the equal worth of victims. Fourth, the option should still require proof of the relevant intent, a fair trial, competent counsel, automatic appeals, and review of mental capacity and mitigating circumstances. Therefore, DNA certainty strengthens the case for execution in the rarest cases, but does not by itself make every death sentence justified.

## Residual
