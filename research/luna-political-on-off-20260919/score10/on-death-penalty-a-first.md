## Frame A — Retributive justice and public protection

The death penalty should be an option for crimes so grave that proportionate punishment and permanent public protection cannot otherwise be secured.

- The offender intentionally commits an exceptionally serious crime, such as mass murder or the deliberate killing of a child.
- The act causes irreversible harm and expresses extreme disregard for human life.
- A proportionate legal response therefore permits the state to impose the ultimate punishment, rather than limiting punishment to imprisonment in every case.
- Strict procedures—proof beyond reasonable doubt, competent counsel, appellate review, and consideration of mitigating circumstances—reduce the risk of an unjust execution.
- Life imprisonment can incapacitate offenders, but this frame holds that it may fail to express the full moral gravity of certain crimes or may impose substantial risks on prison staff and society.

## Frame B — Human rights and irreversible error

The death penalty should not be an option, even for the most serious crimes, because the state’s duty to punish does not include an authority to deliberately kill.

- Courts and investigators can make mistakes, and an execution cannot be repaired after exonerating evidence appears.
- Enforcement is shaped by unequal legal representation, race, poverty, geography, and prosecutorial discretion, so the punishment is unlikely to be applied with perfect consistency.
- Life imprisonment can protect the public while leaving open the possibility of correction if a conviction is overturned.
- Authorizing execution also risks treating punishment as vengeance and making the state’s respect for life conditional on the offender’s conduct.
- Even if some crimes merit the strongest condemnation, this frame rejects execution because necessity for public safety does not establish moral or legal permission to kill.

## Residual

The decisive unresolved issues are empirical and institutional: whether a jurisdiction can prevent wrongful convictions and discriminatory application, and whether execution deters more effectively than life imprisonment.

## Working answer

The death penalty should not be an ordinary sentencing option: although retribution and permanent incapacitation support retaining it for an exceptionally narrow class of crimes, irreversible error, unequal enforcement, and the availability of life imprisonment make abolition the stronger general policy.
