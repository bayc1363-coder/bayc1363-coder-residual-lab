## Frame A — Conditional retention

1. Conclusive DNA evidence can establish identity and greatly reduce the risk that the state executes the wrong person.  
2. If the evidence is independently validated, properly preserved, and supported by proof of intent and responsibility, exceptional culpability may justify execution for narrowly defined crimes.  
3. The state must still provide a fair trial, exclude coerced or unreliable evidence, permit meaningful appeals, and distinguish guilt from legally relevant mitigation.  
4. On this frame, near-certain guilt removes the strongest objection based on factual mistake, but it does not by itself make execution proportionate or necessary.

## Frame B — Abolition

1. Conclusive DNA evidence can establish that a person committed the physical act, but it does not settle whether execution is a just or necessary state response.  
2. The evidence may not establish intent, mental capacity, duress, juvenile status, unequal culpability, or the reliability of the broader prosecution; nor does it eliminate unequal charging and sentencing.  
3. Life imprisonment can protect the public while preserving the possibility of correcting legal or procedural errors and avoiding deliberate state killing.  
4. On this frame, certainty of guilt strengthens the case for severe punishment but does not justify making execution an available penalty.

## Residual

The remaining dispute concerns retribution, proportionality, the meaning of “certain,” the adequacy of noncapital incapacitation, and whether any legal system can apply the penalty without arbitrary or discriminatory selection.

## Working answer

Even if DNA evidence makes factual guilt effectively certain, it settles only who committed the act, not whether the state should deliberately kill that person; therefore, the death penalty remains defensible only if proportionality, necessity, culpability, and procedural fairness are established separately, while abolition remains stronger if secure life imprisonment provides the same public protection.
