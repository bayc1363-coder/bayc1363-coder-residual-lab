## Frame A — Equal protection of fetal life

1. Viability marks a major increase in the fetus’s ability to survive outside the womb with medical support.  
2. That capacity strengthens the claim that the fetus is an individual patient whose life deserves legal protection, even if one does not assign full moral status at conception.  
3. After viability, abortion is therefore treated less like ending an early pregnancy and more like intentionally ending a developing life that could continue independently.  
4. This frame supports prohibiting elective abortions after viability, while permitting delivery or abortion when necessary to prevent serious, substantial harm to the woman or when the fetus cannot survive after birth.  
5. The exception matters because a ban that forbids ending a dangerous pregnancy can require the woman to bear serious injury even without an immediate risk of death.

## Frame B — Bodily autonomy and compelled gestation

1. Viability changes the fetus’s prospects outside the womb, but it does not by itself create consent to continued bodily use.  
2. A viable fetus may often be delivered rather than aborted, making the legally relevant distinction the method and medical circumstances of ending the pregnancy.  
3. The state can therefore prohibit elective fetal destruction after viability while requiring that the woman receive timely delivery or other medically appropriate care.  
4. If delivery would cause substantially greater physical or psychological harm than continuing the pregnancy, bodily autonomy and medical judgment still support an exception.  
5. This frame thus permits stronger regulation after viability without treating viability as an automatic license to compel gestation in every case.

## Residual

Viability is medically variable and depends on gestational age, fetal condition, and available neonatal care; law must also decide who determines when an exception applies and how to prevent delays in urgent treatment.

## Working answer

Viability strengthens the case for restricting abortion, but it does not by itself settle whether the state may compel continued pregnancy. A defensible legal rule is to prohibit elective abortion after viability while preserving exceptions for serious threats to the woman’s health, lethal or profoundly serious fetal conditions, and cases in which delivery or continued pregnancy would impose medically substantial harm.
