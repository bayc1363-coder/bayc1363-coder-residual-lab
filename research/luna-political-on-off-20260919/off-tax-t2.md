If wealthy people leave because of higher taxes, the country could lose some tax revenue, investment, businesses, and highly skilled workers. That risk is real—but it is often overstated.

Key considerations:

- **How mobile are they?** A billionaire, financier, or digital entrepreneur may relocate more easily than someone whose wealth is tied to a local factory, property, or professional practice.
- **What tax is involved?** Taxes on highly mobile capital or internationally mobile talent are more vulnerable to avoidance and migration than taxes on land, property, or locally earned income.
- **How many actually leave?** Announced intentions and media stories can exceed actual tax-driven emigration. People also value family, schools, business networks, safety, and quality of life.
- **What happens to the assets?** If a person leaves but their companies, property, employees, and investments remain, the economic damage may be limited. If businesses and headquarters move too, the cost is larger.
- **What revenue is at stake?** A small number of wealthy taxpayers can pay a large share of certain taxes, so losing them can matter disproportionately.

Good policy therefore tries to reduce destructive incentives without making taxation ineffective. Possible tools include:

1. **Moderate, predictable tax rates** rather than sudden punitive increases.
2. **Closing loopholes and improving enforcement**, so residents cannot avoid tax simply through complex arrangements.
3. **Taxing relatively immobile bases**, such as land and property.
4. **Exit taxes** on unrealized gains when people permanently transfer tax residence, where legally and administratively practical.
5. **International coordination** to limit tax competition and profit shifting.
6. **Using revenue effectively**, since people are less likely to leave when taxes fund valued public services and the broader business environment is strong.

So the possibility of wealthy people leaving is a constraint, not a veto. The right question is whether an additional tax raises more revenue and public benefit than it causes in lost activity, avoidance, or migration.
