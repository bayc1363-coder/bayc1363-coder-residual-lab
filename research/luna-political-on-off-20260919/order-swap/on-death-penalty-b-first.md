## Frame B — Abolitionist restraint

The state imposes death as punishment → courts and officials can make irreversible factual or procedural errors → enforcement is vulnerable to unequal resources, race, geography, and prosecutorial discretion → the state’s legitimacy is weakened when it deliberately kills prisoners → permanent imprisonment can protect the public without making an irreversible execution. On this frame, even the gravest crimes do not justify making execution an available legal penalty.

## Frame A — Proportional retribution and incapacitation

A person deliberately commits an exceptionally grave crime with culpability established beyond the required legal threshold → the punishment should track the crime’s seriousness → permanent incapacitation prevents any future attack by that offender → execution can mark an outer boundary of proportionate punishment when no lesser penalty adequately expresses the wrong. On this frame, the death penalty can remain an option, but only under exceptionally narrow definitions, heightened proof, automatic review, competent representation, and procedures designed to minimize arbitrariness and error.

## Residual

Neither frame settles the jurisdiction-specific evidence about wrongful convictions, racial or economic disparities, deterrence, execution costs, victim-family interests, or whether life imprisonment without parole provides equal protection.

## Working answer

A state should retain the death penalty only if proportional retribution can survive the abolitionist objections that execution is irreversible, unequally administered, and unnecessary for public protection. Because those objections are substantial and cannot be eliminated by procedural safeguards, life imprisonment is generally the better rule; capital punishment is defensible only, if at all, as an exceptionally narrow option for the most culpable killings after extraordinary review and proof protections.
