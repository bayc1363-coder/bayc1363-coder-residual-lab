## Frame B — Limited, liability-based regulation

Corporations should operate primarily under general environmental law, property rights, disclosure duties, and liability for demonstrable harm. The chain is: define prohibited damage → require firms to disclose material risks → let affected parties, investors, consumers, and competitors respond → impose damages or cleanup obligations when harm is established → reserve direct regulation for pollution that markets and courts cannot adequately discipline. This approach treats competition and innovation as mechanisms for reducing environmental costs without giving regulators authority to micromanage every production decision. It also pressures firms to develop cleaner methods because lower waste and energy use can reduce costs and reputational risk. Its condition is that rights, information, enforcement, and remedies must be strong enough to prevent firms from shifting costs onto communities or future generations.

## Frame A — Enforceable environmental standards

Corporations should be regulated because private firms can profit by transferring environmental costs to the public. The chain is: identify ecological thresholds and public risks → set enforceable standards, permits, and limits → monitor emissions and resource use → impose penalties, restoration duties, or liability for violations → revise rules as scientific knowledge and technologies change. Regulation converts environmental damage from an unpriced side effect into a business cost, while predictable standards can reward cleaner innovation and prevent responsible firms from being undercut by polluters. This framework is strongest where harms are cumulative, difficult to reverse, or distributed across people who cannot bargain effectively with the corporation. Its condition is competent administration: poorly designed rules can protect incumbents, impose disproportionate costs, or substitute paperwork for actual ecological improvement.

## Residual

The unresolved issues are empirical and institutional: which standards produce the greatest environmental benefit, how enforcement should be funded, and how costs should be distributed without weakening protection.

## Working answer

Corporations should face enforceable environmental rules, while regulation should rely where possible on disclosure, liability, competition, and flexible incentives rather than detailed control of every business decision. Strong direct standards are justified for irreversible or collective harms; narrower rules are preferable where monitoring, lawsuits, and market pressure can achieve comparable protection. The appropriate policy is therefore regulated accountability: public authorities define ecological limits and enforce them, while corporations retain room to choose the technologies and processes that meet those limits.
