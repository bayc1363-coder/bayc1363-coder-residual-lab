## Frame A — Progressive-capacity frame

If rich taxpayers leave, the policy question becomes whether their departure reflects a genuine fiscal loss or an attempt to escape obligations created under the country’s legal and economic system. Governments can reduce that risk through coordinated taxation, exit taxes on accrued gains, inheritance rules, enforcement against sham residence, and taxes focused on relatively immobile assets such as land and domestic business activity. They can also judge the departure against the public benefits of the tax: revenue, reduced concentration, and broader political legitimacy.

The pressure test is fiscal rather than symbolic. If a small number of departures costs substantial revenue, investment, or employment, the tax design needs adjustment. If departures mainly remove tax privileges while domestic institutions and businesses remain, the threat is weaker. On this frame, allowing wealthy residents to dictate policy through exit would let mobility undermine the shared systems that made their wealth possible.

## Frame B — Incentive-and-limits frame

If rich taxpayers leave, the country loses not only their direct tax payments but also investment, company formation, philanthropy, specialized skills, and future taxable activity. The loss can spread through lower employment, weaker capital markets, and reduced consumption of domestic services. Anticipating that possibility, other wealthy taxpayers can alter their behavior before leaving: they can delay investment, change legal residence, move assets, or devote more resources to tax planning. A rate that appears progressive on paper can therefore collect less revenue than a lower, more stable rate.

The pressure test is the mobility margin. Exit taxes and international coordination can reduce relocation, but aggressive enforcement can also make the country less attractive to entrepreneurs and investors. On this frame, a government should set rates after accounting for the revenue actually collected, the probability of departure, and the economic activity that remains—not after counting the taxable wealth that exists only on paper.

## Residual

The decisive evidence is country-specific: how mobile the affected wealth is, how credible exit-tax enforcement is, and whether departing residents take businesses and investment with them; cross-country comparisons often measure these effects imperfectly.

## Working answer

Rich people leaving does not by itself prove that taxes were too high or that the country should surrender its policy: the right rate depends on whether retaining the tax produces more durable public value than departure destroys in revenue, investment, and economic activity.
