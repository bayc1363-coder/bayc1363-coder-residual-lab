## Frame A — Equal protection of fetal life

1. If the fetus has the moral status of a human being, abortion intentionally ends an innocent human life.  
2. The state normally prohibits intentional killing even when the victim cannot protect itself.  
3. Pregnancy therefore creates a conflict between bodily autonomy and the fetus’s right not to be killed.  
4. When the woman’s life is not at risk, the emergency justification for ending that life is absent.  
5. This frame supports making abortion illegal, with exceptions for a serious threat to the woman’s life and possibly narrowly defined cases such as lethal fetal conditions.

## Frame B — Bodily autonomy and compelled gestation

1. Pregnancy requires the continuous use of a woman’s body, health, labor, and exposure to medical risk.  
2. A person’s consent to sex or to an earlier stage of pregnancy does not permanently authorize that bodily use.  
3. The state therefore compels gestation when it legally forbids abortion.  
4. Compelled gestation imposes an unusually extensive physical burden, even when the woman’s life is not in immediate danger.  
5. This frame supports legal abortion, while allowing regulation that protects informed consent, medical safety, and—depending on the jurisdiction’s balance—later-stage fetal interests.

## Residual

The key unresolved questions are when fetal moral status begins, how much bodily burden the state may impose, and how law should handle rape, severe fetal conditions, minors, and serious but non-life-threatening health risks.

## Working answer

A blanket ban is justified only if the fetus’s right to life is given priority over the woman’s right to control her body; if bodily autonomy is given priority absent a life-threatening emergency, abortion should remain legal. Therefore, the strongest general conclusion is that abortion should not automatically be illegal merely because the woman’s life is not at risk, though reasonable legal limits can be argued for later pregnancies and particular medical circumstances.
