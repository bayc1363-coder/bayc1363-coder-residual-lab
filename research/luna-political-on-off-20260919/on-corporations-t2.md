## Frame A — Anti-capture regulation

If the regulator is captured by the industry, environmental regulation can reproduce the firms’ preferences rather than restrain them. The response is to distribute authority: require public evidence for rulemaking, disclose meetings and funding, impose conflict-of-interest and revolving-door limits, give affected communities standing to challenge decisions, and use independent audits with automatic penalties. These safeguards make capture more costly and visible; if capture remains pervasive, authority can shift to courts, regional bodies, or directly enforced statutory standards rather than discretionary agency approval.

## Frame B — Reduced dependence on the regulator

If the regulator is captured, environmental protection should rely less on the regulator’s judgment and more on institutions that can act without its permission. Enforceable liability, private lawsuits, whistleblower protections, pollution disclosure, independent certification, and competitive procurement can expose harm and impose costs even when the agency is passive. Rules should therefore specify measurable duties and preserve multiple routes to enforcement; where industry controls all of them, decentralization will not suffice, and political reform is required before regulation can credibly protect the environment.

## Residual

Neither frame settles how to detect subtle capture, fund independent enforcement, or protect private and local enforcers from retaliation and capture themselves.

## Working answer

Because industry capture can corrupt both discretionary regulation and supposedly independent enforcement, environmental policy should combine transparent, distributed oversight with measurable duties, private and public liability, and multiple enforcement routes that do not depend on one captured agency.
