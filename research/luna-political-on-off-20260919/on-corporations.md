## Frame A — Collective safeguards

Corporations should be regulated because environmental damage often imposes costs on people who did not consent to the transaction. Firms seeking to minimize private costs can therefore pollute more than is socially acceptable, especially when harms are delayed, dispersed, or difficult to litigate. Binding standards, monitoring, liability, and penalties can make firms account for those costs; predictable rules can then push investment toward cleaner production rather than rewarding the cheapest polluter.

## Frame B — Decentralized discipline

Corporations should be disciplined primarily through enforceable property rights, disclosure, competition, consumer choice, and liability for identifiable harm. When affected parties can obtain information, pursue damages, and compare firms’ environmental performance, market rewards and penalties can redirect capital toward cleaner technologies without requiring regulators to prescribe one production method. Broad command rules can instead protect incumbent firms, invite political capture, or lock in outdated technologies; targeted rules should therefore establish responsibility and transparency while leaving firms room to discover better solutions.

## Residual

Neither frame settles which harms are measurable, how strict standards should be, or how to prevent regulators and corporations from being captured by political or commercial interests.

## Working answer

Corporations should face enforceable environmental constraints, but those constraints should combine minimum standards and liability with disclosure, competition, and flexible mechanisms that let firms find lower-cost ways to reduce harm.
