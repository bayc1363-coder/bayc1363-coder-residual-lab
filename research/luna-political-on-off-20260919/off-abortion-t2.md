Viability strengthens the argument for restricting **elective** abortion, because the fetus may be able to survive outside the uterus and has a greater chance of benefiting from continued protection. Many legal and ethical frameworks therefore permit abortion earlier in pregnancy but limit it after viability, often with exceptions.

However, viability is not a precise switch:

- It varies with gestational age, fetal development, medical resources, and individual circumstances—often discussed around roughly **22–24 weeks**, but with substantial uncertainty.
- A viable fetus still generally cannot survive independently without medical support.
- Serious threats to the woman’s health may exist even when death is not imminent.
- Severe fetal abnormalities may make survival brief or impossible, despite technical “viability.”

If abortion is medically necessary after viability, doctors may sometimes induce labor or perform a cesarean so the fetus can receive care, but that is not always safer or medically appropriate. A legal policy could therefore prohibit non-medically necessary abortions after viability while allowing exceptions for serious health risks, lethal fetal conditions, and possibly rape or incest.

The core disagreement remains: **does viability justify the state overriding the pregnant person’s decision, or does it justify only stronger counseling and regulation?** There is no purely medical answer to that question.
