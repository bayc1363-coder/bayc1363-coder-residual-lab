## Shared

- Both accounts now reject the idea that the offender’s remorse is a necessary permission for forgiveness.
- Both distinguish forgiveness from trust, reconciliation, absolution, and renewed contact.
- Both preserve the injured person’s right to distance, boundaries, consequences, and non-reconciliation.
- Both treat the offender’s lack of remorse as relevant to trust and repair, but not necessarily decisive against forgiveness itself.
- Both require an active distinction between forgiveness and excusing the offense.

## Disputed

- **Account α** treats the absence of remorse primarily as a problem of psychological dependence: the injured person should stop waiting for acknowledgment to regain control of attention and action.
- **Account β** treats it primarily as a moral problem: the injured person may refuse personal vengeance while still denying that the offense has been morally repaired.
- α’s new step is procedural and self-protective: accurately name the harm, stop waiting, release the demand for inner repair, and preserve distance.
- β’s new step is normative and relational: separate the person’s basic human worth from the offense, relinquish vengeance, and withhold reconciliation until remorse and changed conduct appear.
- α can proceed without any continuing orientation toward the offender beyond safety and boundaries. β retains a qualified moral regard for the offender even when repentance is absent.
- Thus, α makes forgiveness more independent of the offender’s future conduct. β makes forgiveness compatible with continued judgment that the relationship is unrepaired and the offender untrustworthy.

## Needs

### Account α: Recovery without permission

For α to succeed on its own terms, the follow-up would need to show that forgiveness can genuinely:

- stop the injured person’s recovery from depending on acknowledgment that may never come;
- reduce the offender’s continuing control over attention, emotion, or action;
- preserve accurate recognition of the harm rather than turn release into denial;
- support protective distance where contact remains unsafe.

The pressure test is whether “release” is actually self-protection or instead premature emotional suppression. α would also be weakened if waiting for remorse were sometimes necessary for the injured person to identify the harm accurately, pursue consequences, or avoid abandoning justified anger before safety is secured. Its account does not by itself settle how much anger is still action-guiding rather than controlling.

### Account β: Mercy without absolution

For β to succeed on its own terms, the follow-up would need to show that one can:

- deny the offense’s acceptability and the offender’s trustworthiness;
- preserve consequences and the possibility of justice;
- refuse personal vengeance without treating the absence of repentance as irrelevant;
- maintain a distinction between basic human worth and moral rehabilitation.

Its pressure test is whether withholding reconciliation while retaining basic regard is a meaningful form of forgiveness or only a more restrained refusal to reconcile. β would also be weakened if assigning basic human worth to an unrepentant offender predictably blurred responsibility or pressured the injured person toward contact. It must therefore keep moral regard separate from restored relationship and from any reduction in consequences.

## Shape

Both avenues stayed inhabited, and both received a substantive new distinction rather than merely repeating the earlier positions.

- **Order:** α still precedes β.
- **Thin slot:** Neither account was reduced to a thin slot; each gained a distinct step and a pressure point.
- **Prestige wrap:** No prestige-based framing is supplied.
- **Omission:** The residual still leaves the practical thresholds for safety, pursuit of consequences, and the choice of the word “forgiveness” underdeveloped.

The working answer still draws on both accounts. If α were deleted, the release from dependence on remorse would lose its clearest basis. If β were deleted, the refusal of vengeance and separation of forgiveness from absolution would lose its moral-repair basis. The synthesis would survive either deletion in a narrower form, but the new packet does not make either account mere scenery.

## Open

- Whether a person can release dependence on remorse without also losing a justified hope for acknowledgment or repair.
- When anger remains an appropriate guide to protection or consequences rather than becoming continued domination by the injury.
- Whether forgiveness without repentance is best described as forgiveness, or instead as release, non-retaliation, or acceptance of non-repair.
- What “basic human worth” requires in practice when the offender remains dangerous or continues offending.
- Whether consequences should be pursued even when doing so sustains attention to the offender.
- Whether non-reconciliation can coexist with the kind of moral repair β describes, or whether β’s account is then limited to preserving the possibility of repair rather than achieving it.
- The texts still do not settle whether forgiveness is owed, advisable, or merely available in a particular case.

## Decision

On this follow-up, there is a modest lean toward **Account α**, based on its own test. The central fact is that the other person is not sorry, and α directly makes forgiveness independent of the offender’s acknowledgment: if release reduces dependence on remorse while preserving accurate judgment and safety, its criterion is met even when no repair is forthcoming.

β remains a coherent account, but its success is more conditional. Its mercy can proceed without repentance, yet the moral-repair dimension is limited when truthful remorse and changed conduct are absent; reconciliation must remain withheld, and repair remains unrealized. The packet therefore supports α as the more immediately sufficient account for this specific case, without establishing that forgiveness is required or safe in every such case.
