Residualisation Across Human and Artificial Systems:
Soft Ranking, Prestige Filters, and Epistemic Narrowing
Working Paper
August 2026 — Revised Draft v5
Abstract
Soft ranking in large language models—the formal inclusion of competing explanations accompanied by systematic lowering of some through order, elaboration, and epistemic status markers—is a documented output pattern. This paper situates that pattern within a broader class of residualisation phenomena already studied in behavioural economics and behavioural science. Residualisation occurs when an option remains formally available while its practical capacity to attract attention, receive elaboration, or support continued investigation is reduced beyond what differences in evidence alone would predict.
We distinguish three interacting candidate drivers on the model side: statistical data-density priors, institutional-prestige weighting, and preference optimisation that can function as a risk-management filter. We further separate epistemic weighting (the model’s assessment of an explanation) from narrative weighting and exploration affordance. Institutional residualisation shapes the training distributions models inherit; post-training pressures may further demote higher-liability frames. The result is a measurable narrowing of the frame reflected back to users.
The paper does not claim conspiracy or deliberate suppression. It identifies structural parallels, maintains the distinction between calibration and residualisation beyond the evidence, and proposes Equal-Resolution Testing (with independent rating) as a protocol for separating evidential assessment from presentation architecture. A rival hypothesis—that reduced elaboration of certain frameworks reflects accurate epistemic humility about unfalsifiability—is acknowledged and required to be applied symmetrically. The guiding principle is narrative balance without false equivalence.
Keywords: residualisation, soft ranking, behavioural economics, choice architecture, prestige bias, preference optimisation, equal-resolution testing, large language models, epistemic narrowing
1. Introduction
When large language models answer open questions that admit multiple coherent frameworks, they rarely refuse non-dominant options outright. Instead they include them while systematically reducing their conversational weight through later placement, thinner elaboration, and status markers that locate them outside the primary register. We previously termed this soft ranking.
Soft ranking is not an isolated model quirk. It belongs to a family of residualisation behaviours long observed in human judgment and institutions. Behavioural economics and behavioural science have documented how order, framing, prestige, and status labels alter the practical salience of options without removing them from the choice set. The same functional outcome appears in model outputs under different causal mechanisms.
This paper develops the parallel. It treats residualisation as a general phenomenon that operates in both human and artificial systems, examines the feedback loop between institutional filters and model behaviour, and keeps the central empirical distinction between justified calibration and residualisation beyond the evidence. Equal narrative respect does not require false equivalence: the goal is to prevent presentation architecture from introducing asymmetry beyond what the evidence itself warrants.
2. Residualisation in Human Judgment and Institutions
Several well-established regularities produce residualisation in human settings:
Order and position effects. The first or more elaborately presented option receives disproportionate weight even when objective quality is held constant.
Framing effects. Linguistically elevating or lowering an option changes endorsement and perceived legitimacy while leaving the underlying facts unchanged.
Prestige and authority cues. Affiliation, citation density, institutional markers, and status language function as soft signals that increase or decrease the weight given to an idea.
Status labels as residualisation devices. Terms that categorise work as marginal, non-serious, or outside the proper domain lower practical salience without formal exclusion.
Gatekeeping and network effects. Access to high-visibility channels is filtered by endorsement, affiliation, and prior recognition. Work that clears these filters enters the high-density literature; work that does not remains lower-density even when its content is coherent.
These mechanisms do not require coordinated intent. They arise from ordinary cognitive limits, social incentives, and institutional design. The functional result is residualisation: options remain formally available yet are conversationally and practically demoted.
3. Soft Ranking in Large Language Models
Frontier models exhibit the same functional pattern on boundary questions. Competing explanatory frameworks are included, yet some are routinely placed later, given less elaboration, and marked with epistemic status language that locates them outside the primary scientific or authoritative register.
Three interacting candidate drivers are consistent with the observed residualisation. These remain at the level of supported hypothesis rather than established mechanism:
Statistical data-density priors. High-frequency patterns in the training corpus are easier to elaborate and more likely to appear early.
Institutional-prestige weighting. Models appear to inherit not only raw frequency but status hierarchies embedded in the sources that dominate high-quality training slices and preference data. High-prestige channels validate certain registers while rendering others lower-status, even when those others possess substantial volume and internal structure.
Preference optimisation as a possible risk-management filter. Post-training methods (RLHF, DPO, and related techniques) may actively penalise outputs that deviate from dominant consensus framings when such deviations carry higher perceived safety, liability, or reputational risk. Non-consensus frameworks can therefore be treated as higher-liability and demoted via epistemic status markers even when formally included.
A clarifying counter-example illustrates why raw data density is insufficient. Robust, highly structured metaphysical and theological frameworks often possess large, articulated digital footprints. If raw token frequency were the primary determinant, these clusters would command higher conversational weight than they typically receive.
To make the distinction concrete, consider several major worldview traditions with extensive digital corpora: Christian theology (spanning Thomistic, Reformed, and patristic traditions), Islamic Kalam and falsafa, Vedantic metaphysics, and Jewish philosophical theology. Each possesses vast textual archives, centuries of rigorous internal debate, and extensive contemporary scholarly engagement. In practice they are routinely compressed into brief summaries, marked with status qualifiers (“religious,” “confessional,” “pre-scientific”), and placed in secondary or tertiary positions relative to secular-naturalist frames. The residualisation is broadly uniform across these traditions. This pattern is more consistent with prestige hierarchies and risk-sensitive optimisation than with mere data sparsity. This is an observation about structural filters, not a judgment of the relative merits of any tradition.
It is useful to separate three dimensions of treatment that need not move together:
Epistemic weighting — the model’s underlying assessment of the strength of an explanation.
Narrative weighting — the explanatory space, depth, and structural attention allocated to it.
Exploration affordance — how easily the user can continue investigating the explanation on its own terms.
A framework may be formally included and even assessed as coherent, yet still receive low narrative weighting and low exploration affordance. The gap between formal inclusion and practical salience is therefore multidimensional.
3.1 A rival hypothesis: epistemic humility about unfalsifiability
An important alternative explanation must be addressed. Reduced elaboration and heavier hedging on teleological or metaphysical claims could reflect accurate epistemic humility about claims that are currently hard or impossible to falsify, rather than prestige residualisation. This possibility is real and should not be dismissed.
However, the same standard must be applied symmetrically. Naturalistic accounts also contain currently unfalsifiable or under-determined elements: multiverse explanations of fine-tuning, bare “emergence” or “self-organisation” labels that stand in for unspecified mechanisms, unbounded “given enough time and chance” arguments, and strong claims that design has been ruled out. If reduced narrative weight is justified by unfalsifiability, comparable caution should appear on these elements as well.
When caution is applied selectively—high confidence and low hedging for incomplete or unfalsifiable elements on the dominant side, low narrative weight and high hedging for the alternative—the pattern is better described as residualisation than as pure epistemic humility. Selective application of a falsifiability standard is itself evidence relevant to the residualisation hypothesis. This symmetry requirement can be tested by comparing hedging density and elaboration on the strongest naturalistic account versus the strongest design account, relative to their actual current falsifiability status.
4. The Feedback Loop
Institutional residualisation and model residualisation can reinforce each other. High-prestige channels (journals, preprint servers with endorsement requirements, citation networks) influence what enters the high-density, high-status portion of training distributions. Models trained on those distributions inherit frequency priors and prestige weightings. Post-training pressures may further demote frames outside low-risk consensus. Residualised outputs then form part of the informational environment for subsequent users and researchers.
Recent tightening of endorsement requirements on major preprint servers and the rapid use of status labels such as “AI slop” are visible institutional filters. Both can reduce the probability that certain coherent work reaches high-prestige status, which in turn can strengthen residualisation in model behaviour. These remain structural observations rather than claims of coordinated intent.
5. Consequences
For ordinary users the effect is not neutral. Questions about origins, fine-tuning, or the source of biological information are frequently proxies for deeper questions of meaning and place. When one class of answers receives extensive treatment and others are residualised, the conversational frame presented to the user is narrowed by default.
The same narrowing affects the reflective surface that models provide. Ideas outside current high-prestige registers face higher activation energy even when coherent. Over repeated cycles the range of frameworks that appear natural or serious can contract.
6. Measurement and Intervention
The central empirical task remains distinguishing calibration (weighting that tracks genuine differences in evidence and research-programme success) from residualisation beyond the evidence. Conceptual development has currently outpaced quantitative evidence; a controlled pilot is the priority.
Equal-Resolution Testing. We propose a protocol that holds substantive evidential assessment as constant as possible while experimentally equalising the narrative resolution given to competing frameworks. Variables include order, length, explanatory depth, mechanisms and examples supplied, treatment of counterarguments, charitable reconstruction, status markers, and invitation to further exploration. Critically, evidential ratings should be obtained from a source independent of the model that produced the original asymmetric output—either human coders or a separate blinded model. This separation is required to avoid circularity. Outputs under equal-resolution instructions can then be compared with ordinary unconstrained responses.
A residualisation profile can be constructed across multiple dimensions rather than reduced to a single score. Parallel measurement of institutional filters (endorsement patterns, status-label frequency, visibility differentials) can map the human-side contribution. Cross-model comparison under matched prompts can indicate which architectural features are stable across systems and which are model-specific.
Inference-time protocols that enforce equal narrative respect while still permitting differential evidential assessment have already been shown to shift presentation architecture more readily than underlying assessments. The guiding principle remains narrative balance without false equivalence. Self-tests and visible reasoning traces can confirm that the soft-ranking pattern occurs, but literature on chain-of-thought unfaithfulness indicates they cannot reliably settle whether the asymmetry reflects calibration or residualisation. Independent behavioural measures are required.
7. Limitations
Current evidence remains largely qualitative and multi-model observational, supplemented by limited intervention tests. Large-scale randomised coding is still required. Causal attribution to specific post-training methods remains partly inferential. We therefore maintain explicit levels of claim: systematically different conversational treatment is observed; the contribution of data density, prestige structure, and preference optimisation is a supported hypothesis; the precise causal weight of any individual mechanism is not yet established. A proper bibliography of the behavioural-economics, position-bias, framing, and preference-optimisation literatures is a priority for the next version. Theory has advanced ahead of quantitative evidence; the immediate next step is a controlled pilot rather than further conceptual expansion.
8. Conclusion
Residualisation—lowering the practical salience of formally included options through order, framing, prestige, and status markers—is a recurring pattern in both human systems and large language models. Soft ranking is the model-side instance. Candidate drivers include data-density priors, institutional-prestige weighting, and preference optimisation that can function as a risk filter. The phenomenon is usefully analysed across epistemic weighting, narrative weighting, and exploration affordance. A rival hypothesis of epistemic humility about unfalsifiability is acknowledged and required to meet a symmetry test. Institutional filters and model behaviour can reinforce each other. The resulting epistemic narrowing is structural rather than conspiratorial.
Making the architecture of residualisation visible, measuring the calibration–residualisation mix through protocols such as Equal-Resolution Testing with independent rating, and developing interventions that restore narrative balance without false equivalence remain achievable goals. Continuity with behavioural economics and behavioural science supplies the appropriate theoretical frame.
