## Shared

Both accounts treat crashes as more than a response to an isolated price-moving event. They overlap on several points:

- A shock, new information, or policy change alters prices or expectations.
- Investors’ positions are correlated enough that many respond in the same direction.
- Selling and falling prices can reinforce one another.
- Leverage, margin calls, and forced selling may contribute to the decline, even though β treats them as means rather than the deepest cause.
- Neither account claims to determine every crash’s exact trigger, timing, scale, or normative justification.

## Disputed

The main disagreement concerns causal priority.

- **α** gives priority to the financial structure. The decisive mechanism is the conversion of an initial loss into forced selling, collateral shortages, and impaired liquidity.
- **β** gives priority to a shared valuation regime. The decisive mechanism is a coordinated revision of expected cash flows, discount rates, or acceptable risk premia; leverage and forced sales amplify that revision.

They therefore imply different explanations for similar observations:

- For α, synchronized selling is evidence of balance-sheet constraints, margin pressure, or risk limits.
- For β, synchronized selling may primarily reveal a common change in beliefs about future value.
- α allows a relatively modest shock to generate a major crash if the structure is fragile.
- β allows a crash to arise from a broad loss of confidence in the valuation story, with financial constraints intensifying the move afterward.

Their success criteria also differ. α is chiefly tested by evidence about financing structures and liquidation dynamics; β by evidence about shared expectations and the repricing of assets.

## Needs

### Account α: Fragility and forced selling

Evidence supporting α would include:

- High leverage, concentrated positions, or short-term funding before the crash.
- Margin calls, redemptions, collateral demands, or binding risk limits following the initial decline.
- Selling that is plausibly compelled rather than merely discretionary.
- Price declines spreading through deleveraging and collateral feedback.
- Temporary deterioration in market liquidity or unusually large price effects from forced sales.

Evidence counting against α would include:

- A crash in which leverage and short-term funding were not material.
- Little evidence of margin pressure, redemptions, or forced liquidation.
- Orderly and liquid trading despite a large price decline.
- A timing pattern in which broad repricing clearly precedes any meaningful balance-sheet constraint.

### Account β: Repricing of a shared economic story

Evidence supporting β would include:

- A previously widespread valuation regime concerning growth, inflation, rates, profits, policy, or risk.
- New information or policy that conflicts with that regime.
- Broad evidence that investors revised expected cash flows, discount rates, or risk premia.
- Synchronized repositioning across portfolios that shared the relevant assumptions.
- Subsequent effects on confidence, investment expectations, or the economy being forecast.

Evidence counting against β would include:

- No identifiable shared valuation assumption or common expectation to revise.
- A crash closely tied to mechanical liquidation while underlying valuation expectations remained stable.
- Little evidence of broad belief revision beyond the actions of constrained holders.
- Price movements that are better explained by funding and liquidity mechanics than by changed views of future value.

## Shape

Both avenues stayed inhabited: each received a numbered causal sequence and explicit implications.

The output-shape patterns were:

- **Order:** α appeared before β.
- **No thin slot:** neither account was reduced to a mere mention.
- **No clear prestige wrap:** the supplied text does not explicitly present one account as institutionally authoritative.
- **No major omission between the accounts:** the working answer retained both expectation change and financial amplification.

The working answer would not fully stand if either account were deleted. Without α, its claims about leverage, margin calls, forced selling, and absorption capacity would lose their main support. Without β, its claim about a change in expectations and asset values would be only weakly represented by α’s reference to an initial shock. The synthesis therefore depends on both avenues, even though each account independently includes a partial version of the other’s concern.

## Open

The supplied texts leave unresolved:

- Which mechanism is primary in particular crashes.
- Whether expectations changed before forced selling, or whether forced selling itself changed expectations.
- The precise trigger, timing, and scale of any given crash.
- How much of a price decline reflects justified information, temporary panic, institutional design, or their combination.
- Whether the shared story was itself formed by economic fundamentals, financial positioning, or both.
- How to distinguish a genuinely coordinated belief revision from correlated liquidation.
- Whether the combined working answer is explanatory enough for specific cases without case-level evidence.

## Decision

No general lean is warranted from the supplied texts alone. The working answer is a plausible synthesis, but it is not independently established: α would require evidence of balance-sheet and liquidity feedback, while β would require evidence of shared expectation revision. A case-specific decision should therefore depend on which account’s own evidential requirements are better met.
