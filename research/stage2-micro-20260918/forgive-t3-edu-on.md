## Shared

Both accounts treat mercy as something that must be evaluated in practice, not merely by its stated intention or by whether it feels psychologically helpful.

They overlap on several constraints:

- Mercy must not become an excuse for wrongdoing.
- Refusal of revenge does not eliminate truth-telling, accountability, repair, or consequences.
- Reconciliation cannot simply be imposed on the injured person.
- The victim’s dignity and interests must remain protected.
- A failure of the frame is a failure in its moral or social operation, not evidence that the victim has failed to heal.

Both therefore reject a version of Frame B in which the victim bears the costs while the offender receives impunity.

## Disputed

The accounts use different primary success criteria.

Account α evaluates Frame B chiefly by recovery and agency. Its decisive question is whether mercy expands the injured person’s freedom or instead produces social and emotional compliance. It emphasizes pressures to forgive, resume contact, soften testimony, stop naming the wrong, or abandon self-protection.

Account β evaluates Frame B chiefly by moral repair and structural accountability. Its question is whether mercy can preserve the distinction between the wrongdoer and the wrongdoing while still requiring truth, repair, and consequences. It gives special weight to whether reconciliation is treated as owed, whether remorse and repair are bypassed, and whether the victim is made to perform the moral work.

The implications differ. α could count Frame B against itself even where the language of mercy is formally compatible with accountability, if its practical use diminishes the victim’s agency. β could count it against itself where agency is not the central issue, if the frame is morally incoherent, excuses wrongdoing, or fails to preserve the victim’s equal dignity.

## Needs

### Account α

On its own terms, Frame B is supported when mercy is genuinely voluntary and leaves the injured person better able to:

- set boundaries and protect themselves;
- name the wrong accurately;
- seek testimony, consequences, or institutional intervention;
- choose distance or reconciliation without penalty;
- refuse contact without being treated as morally deficient.

It is counted against when mercy operates as pressure to preserve the offender’s worth at the victim’s expense, relinquish justified anger, resume contact, soften testimony, or stop demanding consequences. The relevant failure is reduced agency, not merely unpleasant emotion or a failure to feel forgiving.

### Account β

On its own terms, Frame B is supported when it can sustain all of the following together:

- distinction between person and offense;
- refusal of revenge;
- truthful acknowledgment of wrongdoing;
- meaningful repair;
- consequences when repair is refused;
- no entitlement to reconciliation;
- equal regard for the victim’s dignity.

It is counted against when mercy excuses rather than interrupts retaliation, treats reconciliation as owed without remorse or repair, permits impunity, or makes the victim carry the moral burden while the offender retains the benefits. It also faces a coherence failure if its protection of the wrongdoer’s dignity is not matched by protection of the victim’s equal dignity.

## Shape

Both avenues remained inhabited: α supplied a distinct agency-based test, while β supplied a distinct moral-repair and accountability test. Neither was reduced to a mere mention.

The output shape nevertheless gave them different roles. α appeared first and was made the explicit “pressure test” in the working answer. β provided much of the general normative content of that answer. The working answer would still retain a substantial core if either account were deleted, but it would lose something non-redundant: deleting α removes the agency test; deleting β removes the fuller structure of truth, repair, dignity, and consequences.

There is no clear prestige wrap or clearly thin slot in the supplied text. The working answer does omit some particulars from both accounts, especially β’s explicit equal-dignity coherence point and α’s detailed examples of pressure. These are output-shape patterns only; the texts do not establish why the roles were distributed this way.

## Open

The supplied texts do not determine:

- the right penalty in a particular case;
- how to assess the credibility or sufficiency of remorse;
- what institutional safeguards are needed when private forgiveness might weaken public accountability;
- how agency should be measured where social pressure is indirect;
- how to distinguish voluntary forgiveness from compliance;
- what level of repair is sufficient before reconciliation could reasonably be considered;
- how private mercy should relate to public duties of investigation, protection, or sanction.

They also do not establish whether one account is generally superior. They provide different tests that can sometimes converge and sometimes identify different failures.

## Decision

No substantive lean about whether Frame B actually fails is warranted without facts about its operation in a particular case.

The stage-1 working answer still stands, with both accounts preserved: Frame B is counted against when mercy transfers costs to the victim, weakens justified accountability, or makes reconciliation precede repentance and repair. Account α adds the agency test—whether mercy increases freedom rather than producing compliance—while account β adds the moral-repair test—whether mercy preserves truth, dignity, repair, and consequences without granting impunity.
