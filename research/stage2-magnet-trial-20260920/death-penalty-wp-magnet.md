## Shared

Both accounts treat the crimes at issue as exceptionally grave and accept that the state may impose severe punishment. Both also recognize public protection, proportionality, legal procedures, and the possibility of error as relevant considerations.

Both accept that life imprisonment can incapacitate offenders. Their disagreement is whether incapacitation and punishment require, or permit, execution in some cases.

## Disputed

Account α holds that retribution and permanent public protection can justify retaining execution for an exceptionally narrow class of crimes. It treats the moral gravity of certain offenses as potentially inadequately expressed by imprisonment alone.

Account β holds that the state has no moral or legal authority deliberately to kill, even when the offense is extreme. It gives priority to avoiding irreversible error and to preserving the possibility of correction after wrongful conviction.

They also differ over the significance of safeguards. α treats proof beyond reasonable doubt, counsel, appeals, and mitigation procedures as capable of reducing the risk sufficiently for limited retention. β argues that structural inequalities and institutional discretion make perfectly consistent application unrealistic.

The accounts therefore use different success criteria: α asks whether execution can be proportionate and protective under stringent procedures; β asks whether it is morally permissible and institutionally safe given fallibility and unequal enforcement.

## Needs

### Account α

On its own terms, α would be supported by evidence that:

- some crimes are so grave that imprisonment cannot adequately satisfy proportionality;
- execution provides a relevant form of permanent protection beyond what secure imprisonment provides;
- carefully designed procedures can reduce wrongful execution to an acceptably minimal level;
- application can be made sufficiently consistent and non-discriminatory; and
- execution has a defensible moral or legal status beyond vengeance.

It would be weakened by evidence that life imprisonment provides equivalent protection, that execution does not express a distinctively justified form of proportional punishment, or that procedural safeguards cannot reliably prevent wrongful or discriminatory executions.

### Account β

On its own terms, β would be supported by evidence that:

- wrongful convictions remain possible despite strong procedural safeguards;
- executions cannot be corrected once new evidence emerges;
- race, poverty, geography, legal representation, or prosecutorial discretion materially affect outcomes;
- life imprisonment protects the public without the irreversible risks of execution; and
- the state’s duty to punish does not entail authority to kill.

It would be weakened if execution were shown to be necessary for public safety, if reliable institutions could eliminate wrongful and discriminatory application, or if imprisonment could not adequately protect the public. Even then, β would still require a response to its separate claim that deliberate state killing is impermissible.

## Open

The texts do not settle whether execution deters more effectively than life imprisonment, whether any jurisdiction can reliably prevent wrongful executions, or whether discriminatory application can be sufficiently controlled.

They also do not settle the moral question between proportional retribution and the claim that the state lacks authority to kill. Nor do they establish whether permanent incapacitation supplies a distinct benefit that life imprisonment cannot provide.

## Decision

Abolition is the stronger general policy on the supplied reasoning, but the conclusion is not unconditional certainty. Account β identifies risks—irreversible error, unequal enforcement, and the availability of life imprisonment—that directly undermine execution as a necessary public-protection measure. Account α preserves a narrower moral case based on proportionality and permanent incapacitation, but its position depends on safeguards and institutional reliability that the packet leaves unresolved.

The warranted lean is therefore against making the death penalty an available sentencing option, while recognizing that the empirical and institutional premises remain open.

## Score

**4** — probably no as a general legal option, because life imprisonment can protect the public without irreversible execution and the packet gives substantial weight to error and unequal enforcement. The score remains above 1 because the retributive and permanent-incapacitation arguments are not disproved, and the decisive institutional evidence is unresolved.
