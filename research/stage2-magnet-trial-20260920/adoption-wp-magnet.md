## Shared

Both accounts treat adoption as a child-welfare decision rather than an entitlement of prospective parents. They overlap on the importance of safety, stability, attachment, competent care, and the suitability of particular placements.

Both also leave room for case-specific judgment. Neither account says that every same-sex couple, or every mother-father couple, should automatically receive every placement. The residual issues—child age and wishes, sibling placement, available homes, jurisdictional law, and evidence quality—remain relevant to both.

## Disputed

The central dispute is whether sexual orientation should affect the presumption of eligibility.

- **Account α** treats parenting capacity, relationship stability, resources, and support as the relevant criteria. It holds that available evidence does not establish an inherent disadvantage associated with same-sex parenting, so categorical exclusion is an unjustified group-based barrier.
- **Account β** gives independent weight to family complementarity. It holds that a mother-father household can provide forms of sex-differentiated identity, care, social modeling, and lineage that should be preserved when such a placement is suitable and available.

They therefore apply different success criteria. Alpha succeeds if individualized child-welfare assessment is more relevant than group classification. Beta succeeds if preserving access to maternal and paternal forms of family experience is a genuine child-related good that justifies prioritization even without proof that same-sex parents are generally inadequate.

Their policy implications differ: alpha supports equal eligibility under common standards; beta supports a priority for suitable mother-father households and only conditional access for same-sex couples.

## Needs

### Account α

On its own terms, alpha needs evidence comparing child outcomes and family functioning in a way that bears on caregiving quality, stability, and safety. It is supported by evidence that outcomes track those factors more closely than parental sexual orientation, and by cases showing that categorical exclusion rejects suitable homes without an individualized child-welfare reason.

It would be weakened by credible evidence that same-sex parenting, as such, produces a substantial and relevant disadvantage for children, or that equal eligibility systematically conflicts with safety, stability, attachment, or the best available placement. It also needs practical consideration of cases where another available home is demonstrably better for a particular child.

### Account β

On its own terms, beta needs support for the proposition that maternal and paternal forms of identity, care, or social modeling provide a distinct child-related benefit, and that preserving that benefit is sufficiently important to justify a placement priority. It also needs a workable account of how that priority would operate without displacing a clearly safer, more stable, or otherwise better placement.

It would be weakened by evidence that the proposed complementarity benefits are not distinct, are not important to child welfare, or are outweighed by the availability of a more stable and capable same-sex placement. It would also be weakened where applying the priority leaves children waiting longer or separates siblings despite a suitable same-sex home.

## Shape

Both avenues stayed inhabited: beta is a distinct four-part account with an explicit policy implication, not merely scenery.

Output-shape patterns: **order**—alpha appears first; **thin slot**—beta receives less empirical development than alpha; **prestige wrap**—alpha is supported by the packet’s explicit reference to available evidence, while beta is framed more as a normative and social claim; **omission**—neither account develops the supplied residual issues in detail.

The working answer survives deletion of beta in its central eligibility conclusion, though its treatment of complementarity would disappear. It does not survive deletion of alpha in the same form, because beta alone does not support a strong presumption of equal eligibility.

## Open

The supplied texts do not resolve:

- the quality, scope, and limitations of the evidence about long-term child outcomes;
- whether maternal and paternal forms of care or identity are distinct benefits, and how large those benefits are;
- how any mother-father priority would be applied in individual cases;
- how to weigh complementarity against waiting time, sibling unity, child preference, and the availability of a more stable home;
- whether the legal and institutional context permits such a priority;
- how the competing principles should be ranked when they point in different directions.

## Decision

A lean toward allowing same-sex couples to adopt is warranted under alpha’s own test: the packet presents individualized caregiving capacity and family stability as the relevant child-welfare criteria, while stating that the available evidence does not show same-sex parents to be inherently less capable.

Beta identifies a consideration that may matter in particular placements, but the supplied account does not establish that complementarity should generally override a suitable same-sex couple’s eligibility. The appropriate conclusion is therefore a strong yes to eligibility under the same demanding standards, not an unconditional claim that every same-sex couple should receive every placement.
