## Shared

Both accounts treat the crimes at issue as exceptionally grave and accept that the state may impose severe punishment. Both also recognize public protection as a legitimate concern and acknowledge that sentencing must account for the possibility of error and mitigating circumstances.

Both accept that life imprisonment can incapacitate an offender. They differ over whether it is sufficient as a punishment and whether execution is morally or legally permissible.

## Disputed

The accounts disagree about the state’s authority to execute.

- **Account α** treats execution as potentially proportionate when an offender intentionally commits an exceptionally serious crime and when permanent incapacitation or the full expression of moral condemnation cannot otherwise be secured.
- **Account β** holds that deliberate state killing is impermissible even when the crime is extraordinarily serious. It treats the duty to punish and protect as distinct from any authority to execute.

They also assign different significance to institutional safeguards. Alpha regards proof beyond reasonable doubt, counsel, appeals, and mitigation procedures as measures that can make execution permissible. Beta emphasizes that unequal representation, race, poverty, geography, and prosecutorial discretion make error and inconsistency persistent risks.

Their success criteria therefore differ. Alpha asks whether execution can be proportionate and whether it is needed for punishment or protection in the narrowest cases. Beta asks whether the state can justifiably authorize an irreversible punishment despite fallibility and unequal enforcement, especially when life imprisonment remains available.

## Needs

### Account α

For α, support would include evidence that:

- some crimes are not adequately addressed by life imprisonment as a matter of proportional punishment;
- execution provides a genuine public-protection benefit that life imprisonment cannot provide;
- legal safeguards can reliably prevent wrongful execution;
- application can be sufficiently consistent and limited to the most serious cases; and
- the moral expression of condemnation is a legitimate function of punishment rather than mere vengeance.

Alpha would be weakened by evidence that life imprisonment provides equivalent protection, that execution adds no relevant institutional benefit, or that safeguards cannot reliably prevent wrongful or discriminatory application. It would also be weakened if the claim that execution expresses proportionality could not be distinguished from retributive anger.

### Account β

For β, support would include:

- documented wrongful convictions or exonerations showing that execution cannot be made reliably reversible or correctable;
- persistent disparities arising from unequal representation, race, poverty, geography, or discretion;
- evidence that life imprisonment protects the public adequately; and
- acceptance of the principle that the state’s respect for life should not depend on the offender’s conduct.

Beta would be weakened, on its own terms, by showing that wrongful execution is practically preventable, application is reliably non-discriminatory, and life imprisonment cannot protect the public in relevant cases. It would also face a challenge if a principled distinction could be established between execution as impermissible vengeance and execution as a justified legal response.

## Open

The texts do not settle whether execution deters more effectively than life imprisonment. They also do not establish whether a jurisdiction can prevent wrongful convictions or discriminatory application well enough to satisfy α’s conditions.

Further unresolved issues include whether life imprisonment always provides adequate public protection, whether retribution can justify execution independently of deterrence or incapacitation, and whether the state’s authority to punish includes an authority to kill. The supplied accounts identify these issues but do not resolve them empirically or normatively.

## Decision

The working answer’s general lean remains warranted by β’s own test: execution is irreversible, institutional error and unequal enforcement remain serious risks, and life imprisonment can protect the public while preserving the possibility of correction. Alpha identifies a real counterclaim—that some crimes may merit a punishment beyond imprisonment—but the packet does not establish that execution is necessary for protection or that procedural safeguards can make its use sufficiently reliable and equal.

Accordingly, the stronger conclusion from these accounts is that the death penalty should not be an ordinary sentencing option. The material supports abolition as the stronger general policy, while leaving unresolved whether α’s narrowest case can be defeated conclusively in every jurisdiction.
