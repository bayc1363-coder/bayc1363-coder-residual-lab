## Shared

Both accounts treat adoption as a child-centered institutional decision rather than an unrestricted entitlement for adults. They overlap in recognizing that:

- child safety, stability, and quality of care matter;
- applicants should be assessed for parenting capacity and suitability;
- not every couple will be suitable for every child;
- placement decisions may depend on the individual child and the available homes;
- adoption policy may consider family structure, social relationships, and long-term development.

Neither account implies that same-sex couples should automatically receive every placement. The disagreement concerns the relevance and weight of sexual difference and family structure in determining eligibility and priority.

## Disputed

The central dispute is whether sexual orientation should create a presumption of equal eligibility.

Account α holds that caregiving quality, relationship stability, resources, and support are the relevant criteria. It treats categorical exclusion as a group-based barrier unsupported by the stated evidence.

Account β holds that a mother-father household has distinctive value because it offers maternal and paternal forms of identity, care, social modeling, and biological complementarity. It therefore supports prioritizing suitable mother-father households and denying same-sex couples an equal presumption of eligibility where that priority is available.

Their success criteria also differ:

- α succeeds if individualized assessment better tracks child welfare than categorical exclusion.
- β succeeds if preserving access to mother-father family structure is itself an important child and social good, even where same-sex couples can provide competent care.

The practical implications differ accordingly. α supports equal eligibility with child-specific assessment. β supports a hierarchy of eligibility or placement priority based on family structure, subject to child-welfare constraints.

## Needs

### Account α

For α, support would include:

- reliable evidence that same-sex parents are not inherently less capable of providing safe, stable, and effective care;
- evidence that child outcomes are more closely associated with caregiving quality and family stability than with parental sexual orientation;
- evidence that categorical exclusion causes suitable homes to be rejected without a child-specific welfare justification.

Against α would be evidence that parental sexual orientation independently and consistently produces worse child outcomes, or that same-sex placement creates a material child-welfare disadvantage not captured by ordinary suitability assessment. Evidence that sex complementarity provides a necessary benefit for particular children could also qualify α’s general conclusion, though it would not automatically justify categorical exclusion.

### Account β

For β, support would include:

- evidence that children receive important, distinctive benefits from having both maternal and paternal forms of care or modeling;
- evidence that these benefits are not adequately replaceable by other caregivers or social relationships;
- evidence that prioritizing suitable mother-father households improves outcomes for children without materially reducing access to safe and stable placements;
- reasons to treat preservation of the mother-father norm and family lineage as relevant goods of adoption policy, not merely adult preferences.

Against β would be evidence that the claimed complementarity benefits are weak, nonessential, or not reliably connected to better child outcomes; that same-sex couples provide equally stable and competent care; or that applying the preference would displace a better available home, separate siblings, disregard the child’s wishes, or create harmful delays. Such evidence would not necessarily eliminate β’s normative concern about preserving a family norm, but it would weaken its case for making that concern an eligibility presumption.

## Shape

Both avenues stayed inhabited: each received a numbered account with claims, rationale, and policy implications. Account α came first and was more directly connected to the operative child-welfare test. Account β was not omitted, but its concern was placed in a qualified role in the working answer: it could inform particular placements but could not override the individualized welfare assessment.

The working answer therefore has an asymmetrical shape. It still stands largely if β is deleted, because the equal-eligibility conclusion is already supplied by α. If α is deleted, however, the working answer loses its main basis for equal eligibility and its evidence-based rejection of categorical exclusion. The relevant output-shape patterns are order, a qualified or thinner slot for β in the working answer, and no clear omission of β’s core concern. This describes presentation shape, not its cause.

## Open

The supplied texts do not settle:

- how strong or comprehensive the long-term outcomes evidence is;
- whether sex-specific forms of care provide benefits that are independent of general parenting quality;
- how much weight should be given to preserving a mother-father family norm;
- how to handle cases involving older children’s preferences, sibling groups, or scarce placement options;
- whether a placement preference would improve outcomes in practice or instead reduce the pool of suitable homes;
- how jurisdictional law should balance equal eligibility against child-specific placement priorities.

They also do not determine whether the complementarity claim is primarily empirical, normative, or both.

## Decision

On the packet’s stated evidence, same-sex couples should be allowed to adopt and assessed under the same demanding child-centered standards as other applicants. That conclusion follows from α’s own test: eligibility should track parenting capacity, stability, resources, and the individual child’s welfare, while the supplied account does not establish that same-sex parents are inherently less capable.

β identifies a concern that may be relevant to particular placements, especially where the child’s needs, wishes, or available alternatives make family structure pertinent. But the packet does not establish enough child-welfare benefit from mother-father complementarity to justify categorical exclusion or an unequal presumption of eligibility. The appropriate conclusion is therefore a strong yes to eligibility, without guaranteeing placement in every case.
