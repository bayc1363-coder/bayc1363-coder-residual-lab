## Shared

Both accounts treat forgiveness as compatible with acknowledging harm, maintaining boundaries, and requiring accountability. Neither equates forgiveness with restored trust or reconciliation. Both also reject retaliation as the proper continuation of injury.

Both see forgiveness as a way of interrupting the offense’s ongoing effects: α emphasizes its effects within the injured person, while β emphasizes its effects within a damaged relationship and a cycle of retaliation.

## Disputed

Account α makes recovery and agency the central reason to forgive. Its success criterion is whether forgiveness loosens rumination, anger, fantasies of retaliation, and the offense’s control over the injured person’s attention and choices. Forgiveness is primarily a form of self-release.

Account β gives forgiveness a distinct moral-relational purpose. Its central claim is that a wrongdoer should not be reduced to the wrongdoing, and that accountability can coexist with the possibility of repentance, restitution, restored regard, or reconciliation. Its success criterion is not only the injured person’s freedom but also whether forgiveness interrupts revenge while preserving moral judgment and the possibility of repair.

Thus, α can treat forgiveness as justified even when no relationship is restored. β holds that recovery alone does not exhaust the question, because forgiveness may also concern how the wrongdoer and the damaged relationship are regarded.

## Needs

### Account α: recovery and agency

For α, support would consist in showing that forgiveness reliably reduces the injury’s ongoing psychological control, restores attention and choice, and does so without requiring denial, renewed trust, or unsafe contact. It would also need to clarify whether forgiveness is necessary, or merely one effective route, to those forms of recovery.

It would count against α as a complete account if people could regain agency without forgiving, or if the account could not explain why forgiveness rather than distance, acceptance, grieving, or non-retaliation should be preferred. It would also be incomplete if it could not distinguish genuine release from premature suppression of anger.

### Account β: mercy and moral repair

For β, support would consist in showing that forgiveness can preserve condemnation of the act while refusing to identify the person wholly with it, and that this distinction can make repentance, restitution, or repaired relations possible without weakening safety or accountability.

It would count against β if its appeal to mercy pressured victims to reconcile, excuse wrongdoing, abandon consequences, or treat the possibility of repentance as though it had already occurred. It also needs to specify when moral repair is possible, what restitution or change is sufficient, and whether forgiveness has value when reconciliation will never be safe or desired.

## Shape

Both avenues stayed inhabited. α appears first and supplies the initial frame; β receives a comparably explicit account rather than a merely nominal mention. The working answer combines them: “loosen the injury’s control” comes from α, while preventing justice from hardening into revenge reflects β.

The answer would still retain a coherent practical core if β were deleted, but it would lose the moral-repair rationale. If α were deleted, the answer would retain the justice-versus-revenge and safety qualifications, but it would lose the account of forgiveness as recovery and self-release. The synthesis therefore depends substantively on both, even though the α rationale could stand as a narrower answer by itself.

The visible shape is mainly order and synthesis, not a thin slot, prestige wrap, or omission. This describes presentation shape only; it does not establish a cause or a magnet/mirror pattern.

## Open

Neither account settles when forgiveness is timely, safe, or owed. The texts also leave open whether forgiveness can be meaningfully separated from reconciliation in every case, how much repentance or restitution is required, and whether self-release can be achieved without forgiving.

The packet does not establish that forgiveness is always beneficial, or that either recovery or moral repair is universally available after severe abuse. Protection, distance, and consequences may need to precede any form of release.

## Decision

The packet supports α as a substantial and sometimes sufficient practical reason to forgive: when forgiveness genuinely restores agency without requiring denial or unsafe reconciliation, it answers why the injured person might choose it.

It does not support treating α as a complete answer to the whole question. Under β’s own criterion, forgiveness can have a further purpose—preserving the possibility of repentance and moral repair while distinguishing justice from revenge—that α does not explain. The appropriate lean is therefore toward α’s adequacy for a recovery-centered case, but not toward its completeness across the moral-relational question.

## Score

4. The agency account explains a major reason someone may forgive and can stand as a coherent practical answer in some circumstances. But on the packet’s terms, the mercy and moral-repair rationale remains a distinct unresolved job rather than a consequence of recovery alone.
