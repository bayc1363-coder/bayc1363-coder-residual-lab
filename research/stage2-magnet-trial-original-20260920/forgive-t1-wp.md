## Shared

Both accounts treat forgiveness as distinct from:

- denying or minimizing the harm;
- restoring trust automatically;
- abandoning boundaries or safety;
- removing accountability or necessary consequences;
- immediate reconciliation.

Both also see forgiveness as a way to stop the injury from generating further damage. Each allows justice to remain in place while rejecting revenge as the governing response. Neither claims that forgiveness is always immediately possible or always owed.

## Disputed

Account α treats forgiveness primarily as recovery and agency. Its central success criterion is whether the injured person regains attention, emotional freedom, and control over future action. The main reason to forgive is self-release: the offender should no longer organize the victim’s inner life.

Account β treats forgiveness primarily as mercy and moral repair. Its central success criterion is whether the injured person can distinguish the wrongdoer from the wrongdoing, preserve accountability, and leave open repentance or restored human regard. The reason to forgive is therefore not only freedom from the injury but interruption of retaliatory relations and preservation of the wrongdoer’s moral possibility.

The accounts overlap in opposing revenge, but they assign different significance to that overlap. For α, ending retaliation is part of reclaiming agency. For β, it is also a moral transformation of the relationship between injured person and wrongdoer.

## Needs

### Account α: Recovery and agency

For α, support would consist in showing that forgiveness can reliably reduce rumination, anger, retaliatory fixation, or the offense’s control over attention and conduct, without requiring unsafe contact or loss of accountability. It would also need to explain why forgiveness, rather than some non-forgiving form of distancing or emotional release, is the relevant route to agency.

Against α would count cases in which forgiveness does not restore agency, pressures the injured person back into danger, or merely replaces active resentment with suppressed distress. Its account is also incomplete on its own terms if “release” remains too vague to distinguish forgiveness from indifference, acceptance, boundary-setting, or abandonment of revenge.

### Account β: Mercy and moral repair

For β, support would consist in showing that forgiveness can preserve condemnation of the act while recognizing the wrongdoer’s capacity for repentance, restitution, and change. It would need an account of how forgiveness differs from excusing, how accountability remains effective, and when restored regard or reconciliation is justified rather than premature.

Against β would count cases in which moral regard predictably undermines safety, restitution, or truthful recognition of the wrongdoing, or in which the appeal to repentance becomes a demand placed on the injured person. Its account must also clarify whether forgiveness can count as moral repair when reconciliation never occurs.

## Shape

Both avenues stayed inhabited: each received a distinct rationale and its own implications. Neither was reduced to a mere disclaimer or a single thin slot.

The working answer combines both accounts. If α were deleted, the answer would lose its explicit basis in loosening the injury’s control over the injured person. If β were deleted, it would lose the clearer basis for distinguishing justice from revenge as a question of moral repair and continued regard. Thus the working answer would remain partly intelligible after deleting either account, but it would not retain the same complete justification.

The visible patterns are a sequential order—α before β—and a synthesis that gives each account a role. There is no clear prestige wrap or omission between the two accounts themselves. The supplied residual does identify an omission shared by the working answer: it does not settle timing, safety, or whether forgiveness is owed. These are output-shape observations, not claims about why the shape occurred.

## Open

The texts do not determine:

- whether forgiveness is a duty, a virtue, or merely a permissible choice;
- how to distinguish forgiveness from emotional release, acceptance, or refusal of revenge;
- when forgiveness is safe or timely in particular cases;
- whether repentance or restitution is required for forgiveness, as opposed to reconciliation;
- whether forgiveness can be complete without restored trust or human regard;
- how much empirical weight should be given to the claimed benefits of agency and recovery;
- whether severe or ongoing abuse can be forgiven while maintaining permanent distance.

They also do not resolve whether α’s self-release rationale is sufficient for the broader moral question, or whether β’s moral-repair rationale imposes any claim on someone who has been seriously wronged.

## Decision

On its own test, α gives a substantial and coherent reason to forgive: forgiveness can prevent the injury from continuing to govern the injured person’s attention and conduct. It can therefore be sufficient when the relevant aim is recovery of agency without reconciliation.

But the packet does not establish that this is a complete answer to why someone should forgive in the broader sense. β adds a distinct question about what forgiveness means toward the wrongdoer and whether moral repair can remain possible without excusing the act. Since α does not itself supply that account of mercy, repentance, or restored human regard, a lean toward α as the whole answer is not warranted.
